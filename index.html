Potassium in biochar, by feedstock

Biochar retains most of the potassium (K) in its source biomass, so potassium content varies by feedstock. In this dataset, median wood biochar contains about 2,800 mg K/kg, while median manure biochar contains roughly eleven times as much. This page presents every potassium value the American Biochar Institute has extracted from its reference library, along with the feedstock, measurement reagent, and source study, plus commercial products measured for the USDA-ARS Biochar Atlas. Filters recalculate the statistics, and each figure explains what the data can and cannot support.

This page serves users who need the underlying data rather than a summary, including producers and agronomists assessing applications, researchers, product manufacturers, and nutrient-rule writers. It supports USDA-NRCS Conservation Practice Standard 336, Soil Carbon Amendment, by providing evidence for considering feedstock-specific values in place of the standard's single default for plant-available potassium.

This page shows how much potassium different types of biochar contain, how much may be available to crops, and why the results depend on the feedstock and testing method. Readers can compare the evidence, explore how the values change with different filters, and see the first-year availability factors ABI proposes for nutrient planning.

Feedstock sets the amount; the reagent sets what counts as available

Potassium does not pose the same water-quality concern as phosphorus. The practical issue is how much of a biochar's potassium a nutrient plan can count toward crop needs. The NRCS Soil Carbon Amendment calculator currently credits 100 percent in the first year, assuming biochar behaves like compost. The evidence presented here does not yet support a single value for all biochars:

  • Feedstock largely determines total potassium. Wood biochar is lowest; crop-residue, food-waste, digestate, and manure biochars contain about seven to eleven times more.
  • Recovery varies by reagent: water extracts the least; ammonium acetate (the standard U.S. soil-test reagent) recovers about two fifths; mild acids and salts recover one half to three quarters; and strong acids recover nearly all potassium.
  • Limited studies found that manure biochars released most of their potassium over weeks or months, while wood biochar results varied widely. See the year-one panel below.
  • Because few studies provide paired data, these shares are preliminary, not established coefficients.

What each reagent recovers

Extractable potassium is reported by reagent in mg K per kg of biochar. Because each reagent recovers a different potassium pool, the values are not additive or interchangeable. Water measures the immediately soluble fraction. Ammonium acetate (1 M, pH 7) measures exchangeable potassium and is the standard U.S. soil test, although it is calibrated for soils rather than carbon-rich amendments. Mehlich-3, calcium acetate lactate, CaCl₂ and CAT, and 1 M ammonium nitrate are other soil or substrate tests. Dilute HCl, oxalate, 1 M HCl, and 2 percent citric acid are acid or chelating reagents that dissolve progressively more mineral potassium. Because interpretation depends on the reagent used, every row identifies the extractant. The two pooled rows at the top combine all reagents. This figure responds to the feedstock and wood filters, but not the temperature filter.

How much of a biochar's potassium a reagent recovers

The figure shows extractable potassium as a percentage of total potassium in the same biochar, grouped by feedstock class. As on the phosphorus page, it pools reagents that recover different potassium pools; therefore, the labeled median summarizes results across methods, while the spread is more informative. Strong acids (1 M HCl and 2 percent citric acid) are excluded because they measure mineral potassium rather than the fraction available to crops. Values above 100 percent are retained even though they are physically impossible; incomplete total digestion is one possible explanation, and each such value flags a pair to check. The figure responds to the feedstock and wood filters, but not the temperature filter, because paired values are often reported without temperature data.

ABI's proposed year-one potassium availability factors

ABI proposes these values to replace the calculator's single 100 percent factor. For each feedstock class, ABI takes the median share of total potassium recovered by plant-available reagents (water, ammonium acetate, ammonium nitrate, CaCl₂ or CAT, Mehlich-3, and dilute HCl) across the paired values, then rounds down to the nearest 10 percent. Where crop-uptake evidence suggests that a class gives up most of its potassium within one season, the factor is raised one step; crop residue is the only class where that applies, on one study. Classes with fewer than five pairs use the factor of the most similar class and are marked accordingly. If a laboratory reports ammonium-acetate exchangeable potassium for the biochar itself, that measured value can replace the factor.

How much potassium an application delivers

Potassium applied per acre depends on the biochar's concentration and how much of it goes on: lb K₂O/acre = total K (mg/kg dry) × dry short tons/acre × 0.0024092. This is total potassium, not the share a crop can use; the proposed factors above apply the recovered shares. Where a study did not state its moisture basis, the value is treated as dry, as on the phosphorus page. The two pooled rows follow the feedstock filter.
